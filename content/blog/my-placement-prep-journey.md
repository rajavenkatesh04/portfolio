Final year is a strange kind of pressure: you're finishing a degree, building projects, and preparing for interviews all at once. I decided to stop treating prep as something I'd "get to" and instead run it like a project with a deadline. This is how I structured 20 days across three parallel tracks — and what actually worked.

## The three tracks

I deliberately ran three things at the same time, because interviews test all of them and cramming one at a time wastes the calendar.

1. **DSA / LeetCode** — a structured 20-day plan, topic by topic.
2. **Aptitude & quant** — a small dose every single day, no exceptions.
3. **AWS Cloud Practitioner (CLF-C02)** — a certification to anchor my cloud fundamentals.

## The routine

The whole thing lived or died on routine, not motivation. A representative day looked like this:

- **Morning** — one new DSA topic: learn the pattern, then solve 3–4 problems on it.
- **Midday** — 30–45 minutes of aptitude and quant. Timed, like the real thing.
- **Evening** — an hour of AWS material: a service area, then practice questions.
- **Night** — revisit one problem I'd failed earlier and re-solve it from scratch.

The DSA plan was sequenced so each topic built on the last — arrays and strings before two pointers, recursion before trees and graphs, and so on.

## What worked

- **Spaced repetition on failures.** Re-solving problems I'd previously botched, days later, did more for retention than grinding new ones.
- **A little aptitude every day.** Small and daily beat large and occasional — it kept the mental math sharp without eating a whole session.
- **The certification as a forcing function.** A fixed exam date made the cloud track non-negotiable, which kept it from getting crowded out by DSA.
- **Tracking everything.** A simple sheet of "topic / problems solved / what tripped me up" turned a vague grind into visible progress.

## What I'd do differently

- **Start mock interviews earlier.** I treated them as a final step; they should have been a weekly checkpoint from day one.
- **Fewer easy problems.** Easy problems feel productive and rarely teach you anything past the first few.
- **Protect rest.** The days I skipped sleep to add another hour were the least useful days. Tired reps are wasted reps.

## The takeaway

Twenty days isn't long, but a structured twenty beats an unstructured two months. Running the tracks in parallel felt heavier day to day, yet it matched how interviews actually work — they don't ask you to be good at one thing in isolation.

More than the syllabus, the habit is what stuck: show up daily, track honestly, and revisit what you got wrong. That's the part I carried past placement season.
